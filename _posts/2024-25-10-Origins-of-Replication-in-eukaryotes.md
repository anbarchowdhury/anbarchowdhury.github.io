    <p style='margin-top:0in;margin-right:0in;margin-bottom:8.0pt;margin-left:0in;line-height:115%;font-size:16px;font-family:"Times New Roman",serif;text-align:center;'><strong><span style='font-size:24px;line-height:115%;font-family:"Times New Roman";'>Origin of DNA Replication in Eukaryotes</span></strong></p>
    <p style='margin-top:0in;margin-right:0in;margin-bottom:8.0pt;margin-left:0in;line-height:115%;font-size:16px;font-family:"Times New Roman",serif;text-align:justify;'><strong><span style="font-size: 24px;">Introduction</span></strong></p>
    <p style='margin-top:0in;margin-right:0in;margin-bottom:8.0pt;margin-left:0in;line-height:115%;font-size:16px;font-family:"Times New Roman",serif;text-align:justify;'>All life-forms store their genetic information in the form of nucleic acids and by the process of DNA Replication, the genetic information is passed from one generation to another.</p>
    <p style='margin-top:0in;margin-right:0in;margin-bottom:8.0pt;margin-left:0in;line-height:115%;font-size:16px;font-family:"Times New Roman",serif;text-align:justify;'>DNA replication in eukaryotes starts with a protein called &lsquo;initiator factor&rsquo; which loads the replication machineries onto a particular genomic loci (genome) known as origins (Gilbert, 2004). The number of origin differs between eukaryotes and prokaryotes. Prokaryotes (such as bacteria) have only one origin of replication (oriC) whereas the eukaryotes have multiple origins of replication. Bacteria&rsquo;s chromosome is circular, but eukaryotes have multiple linear chromosome. In eukaryotes, the number of these origins depends on the size of the genome. Larger genomes have more origins compared to smaller genomes and larger genomes have more origins which ensures that the entire DNA is copied efficiently.</p>
    <p style='margin-top:0in;margin-right:0in;margin-bottom:8.0pt;margin-left:0in;line-height:115%;font-size:16px;font-family:"Times New Roman",serif;text-align:justify;'><em>Saccharomyces cerevisiae&nbsp;</em>is the most studied and researched eukaryote. It is a unicellular (single celled) fungus commonly known as budding yeast or just yeast. It has 400 origins in its 12 million base pairs genome whereas <strong>humans&nbsp;</strong>have 30,000 to 50,000 origins in their 3 billion base pairs genome (Leonard &amp; Mechali, 2013). <em>Schizosaccharomyces. pombe&nbsp;</em>is commonly known as fission yeast and is a valuable species for studying DNA replication and cell cycle regulation. It is studied because of its various similarities to many eukaryotes. Metazoans are multicellular animals. This group includes all animals from simple organisms like sponges to complex beings like humans.</p>
    <p style='margin-top:0in;margin-right:0in;margin-bottom:8.0pt;margin-left:0in;line-height:115%;font-size:16px;font-family:"Times New Roman",serif;'>&nbsp;</p>
    <p style='margin-top:0in;margin-right:0in;margin-bottom:8.0pt;margin-left:0in;line-height:115%;font-size:16px;font-family:"Times New Roman",serif;'><strong><span style="font-size:24px;line-height:115%;">Literature Review</span></strong></p>
    <p style='margin-top:0in;margin-right:0in;margin-bottom:8.0pt;margin-left:0in;line-height:115%;font-size:16px;font-family:"Times New Roman",serif;text-align:justify;'>Origins of replication are chromosomal regions that are responsible for the recruitment of initiators. These initiators facilitate the assembly of DNA replication machineries (Francois Jacob, 1963). Generally, the number of origin is closely associated with size of the genome, this ultimately ensures that chromosome duplication can be carried out on a physiologic ally manageable timescale (Gilbert, 2004). In the early stages of embryo development, origins are spaced much closer together than they would be in regular body cells, this makes copying of DNA much faster. As the embryo develops and approaches a stage called the mid-blastula transition (MBT), these replication origins spread out more, fewer of them are used, and DNA copying slows down. (Kermi, Lo Furno, &amp; Maiorano, 2017)</p>
    <p style='margin-top:0in;margin-right:0in;margin-bottom:8.0pt;margin-left:0in;line-height:115%;font-size:16px;font-family:"Times New Roman",serif;'>&nbsp;</p>
    <p style='margin-top:0in;margin-right:0in;margin-bottom:8.0pt;margin-left:0in;line-height:115%;font-size:16px;font-family:"Times New Roman",serif;'>&nbsp;</p>
    <p style='margin-top:0in;margin-right:0in;margin-bottom:8.0pt;margin-left:0in;line-height:115%;font-size:16px;font-family:"Times New Roman",serif;'><strong><em><span style="font-size:21px;line-height:115%;">Saccharomyces cerevisiae</span></em></strong><em><span style="font-size:21px;line-height:115%;">&nbsp;</span></em><strong><span style="font-size:21px;line-height:115%;">Origins of replication</span></strong></p>
    <p style='margin-top:0in;margin-right:0in;margin-bottom:8.0pt;margin-left:0in;line-height:115%;font-size:16px;font-family:"Times New Roman",serif;text-align:justify;'>The origins of <em>S. cerevisiae&nbsp;</em>are known as ARSs (Autonomous Replication Sequences). These regions are AT rich, and they function as the replication start site (Brewer &amp; Fangman, 1987; Huberman et al., 1987,1988) and they also recruit the eukaryotic initiator (Bell &amp; Stillman, 1992). Adenine (A) and thymine (T) bases are abundant in ARSs. An important component of these ARSs is the &quot;A element,&quot; also known as the ARS Consensus Sequence (ACS), an 11-basepair sequence (Marahrens &amp; Stillman, 1992; Rao et al., 1994; Rao &amp; Stillman, 1995; Theis &amp; Newlon, 1994). This sequence is essential because it is where the ORC (Origin Recognition Complex) binds to start the replication process. In <em>S. cerevisiae&nbsp;</em>genome, this ACS starts the replication process. There is more than 12,000 ACS like sequences present but about 400 of them facilitate the replication initiation process (Nieduszynski et al., 2006; Wyrick et al. 2001; Xu et al., 2006).<em>&nbsp;S. cerevisiae&nbsp;</em>origins are nucleosome free regions (NFRs), and they are maintained this way (Berbenetz et al., 2010; Eaton et al., 2010). The arrangement of nucleosomes around the ARS can affect how well the ORC can bind and start replication. If the ORC is removed, nucleosomes can move into the ARS region, making it harder for replication to start.</p>
    <p style='margin-top:0in;margin-right:0in;margin-bottom:8.0pt;margin-left:0in;line-height:115%;font-size:16px;font-family:"Times New Roman",serif;'><strong><em><span style="font-size:21px;line-height:115%;">&nbsp;</span></em></strong></p>
    <p style='margin-top:0in;margin-right:0in;margin-bottom:8.0pt;margin-left:0in;line-height:115%;font-size:16px;font-family:"Times New Roman",serif;'><strong><em><span style="font-size:21px;line-height:115%;">Schizosaccharomyces pombe</span></em></strong><strong><span style="font-size:21px;line-height:115%;">&nbsp;origins of replication</span></strong></p>
    <p style='margin-top:0in;margin-right:0in;margin-bottom:8.0pt;margin-left:0in;line-height:115%;font-size:16px;font-family:"Times New Roman",serif;text-align:justify;'><em>S. pombe&nbsp;</em>origins do contain around 400 origin sequences in their genome &amp; they are nucleosome free (Givens et al., 2012;Xuet al., 2012), they are nucleosome free because it helps the replication machineries to access the DNA more effectively. These origin sequences are rich in adenine (A) &amp; thymine (T). AT-rich regions are easier for unwinding as they contain two hydrogen bonds compared to GC-rich regions which contain three hydrogen bonds. These are approximately 1 kilobase long (Dai et al., 2005; Heichinger et al., 2006; Segurado et al., 2003).</p>
    <p style='margin-top:0in;margin-right:0in;margin-bottom:8.0pt;margin-left:0in;line-height:115%;font-size:16px;font-family:"Times New Roman",serif;text-align:justify;'>The Orc4 subunit of the Origin Recognition Complex (ORC) in <em>S. pombe&nbsp;</em>has a certain function which is species-specific insertion that plays a role in origin selection. This insertion is responsible for encoding the DNA-binding element which ultimately recognizes the AT-rich region on the minor groove (Chuang &amp; Kelly, 1999; Moon et al., 1999).</p>
    <p style='margin-top:0in;margin-right:0in;margin-bottom:8.0pt;margin-left:0in;line-height:115%;font-size:16px;font-family:"Times New Roman",serif;'><strong><span style="font-size:21px;line-height:115%;">&nbsp;</span></strong></p>
    <p style='margin-top:0in;margin-right:0in;margin-bottom:8.0pt;margin-left:0in;line-height:115%;font-size:16px;font-family:"Times New Roman",serif;'><strong><span style="font-size:21px;line-height:115%;">Metazoan origins of replication</span></strong></p>
    <p style='margin-top:0in;margin-right:0in;margin-bottom:8.0pt;margin-left:0in;line-height:115%;font-size:16px;font-family:"Times New Roman",serif;text-align:justify;'>The ORC is <em>S. cerevisiae&nbsp;</em>binds to specific DNA sequences known as the Autonomously Replicating Sequence (ARS). However, metazoan ORC does not rely on specific consensus sequence (Remus et al., 2004; Vashee et al., 2003. This allows DNA replication to be started from a variety of sequences. The metazoan ORC also binds to the nucleosome free region in the genome (Heinzel et al., 1991; Hyrien &amp; Mechali, 1993; Mechali &amp; Kearsey, 1984).</p>

They have two purposes (Huppert &amp; Balasubramanian, 2007; Wong &amp; Huppert, 2009; Fenouil et al., 2012; Cayrou et al., 2011&ndash;2015; Valton et al., 2014):</p>
    <ol style="list-style-type: decimal;">
        <li>NFR maintenance</li>
        <li>The Formation of G-quadraplex.</li>
    </ol>
    <p style='margin-top:0in;margin-right:0in;margin-bottom:8.0pt;margin-left:0in;line-height:115%;font-size:16px;font-family:"Times New Roman",serif;text-align:justify;'>In metazoans, once the ORC starts loading the MCM2-7 helicase onto the DNA, the helicase can change its position by moving away from the ORC binding site (Evrin et al., 2009; Remus et al., 2009). The movement of helicase can occur by either diffusion or other cellular processes such as transcription (Edwards et al., 2002; Gros et al., 2015; Powell et al., 2015; Ritzi et al., 1998). Thus, this ultimately indicates that in the metazoans the initial ORC binding is not always the position where DNA replication begins.</p>
    <p style='margin-top:0in;margin-right:0in;margin-bottom:8.0pt;margin-left:0in;line-height:115%;font-size:16px;font-family:"Times New Roman",serif;text-align:justify;'>&nbsp;</p>
    <p style='margin-top:0in;margin-right:0in;margin-bottom:8.0pt;margin-left:0in;line-height:115%;font-size:16px;font-family:"Times New Roman",serif;'><strong><span style="font-size:24px;line-height:115%;">Discussion</span></strong></p>
    <p style='margin-top:0in;margin-right:0in;margin-bottom:8.0pt;margin-left:0in;line-height:115%;font-size:16px;font-family:"Times New Roman",serif;text-align:justify;'>DNA replication is the most crucial biological process which takes place across all the life-forms. By this process the transmission of genetic information encoded within the nuclear and mitochondrial DNA from one generation to the next occurs during somatic cell division. Bacteria mostly have circular single or multiple chromosomes with their small genome size. Archaea have circular chromosomes like that of the bacteria. Eukaryotes&rsquo; genome size is much larger than Bacteria &amp; Archaea, they multiple linear chromosome. The process of DNA replication varies across different organisms. The structure and composition of replication origins also vary across species. In <em>S. cerevisiae</em>, origins are known as Autonomous Replication Sequences (ARSs), which are AT-rich regions containing specific sequences that bind the Origin Recognition Complex (ORC). In <em>Schizosaccharomyces pombe</em> (fission yeast), a similar AT-rich composition is observed in replication origins, but with notable species-specific differences. The Orc4 subunit in <em>S. pombe&nbsp;</em>ORC has a unique DNA-binding element that aids in recognizing AT-rich regions, highlighting how origin selection can vary even among closely related species. In more complex eukaryotes, such as metazoans, replication origins are less dependent on specific sequences like ARS and more on broader structural features, such as nucleosome-free regions, G-rich sequences, and CpG islands. This flexibility allows replication to initiate from multiple potential sites.</p>
